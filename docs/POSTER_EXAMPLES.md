## Q: "Will the X-ray hurt?"

**Ages 5–11**
> Sometimes the X-ray might feel a little sharp, but it doesn't hurt at all. The machine just uses really strong light to make a picture of what's inside your body — kind of like how an X-ray works. It doesn't touch you or poke at anything inside your body, so it's completely safe. You might feel a little warmth from the light or see some blurry spots, but that's it!

**Ages 12–18**
> X-rays are a type of electromagnetic radiation that can cause some discomfort depending on the size and position of your body part. If you're anxious about it, ask your nurse or doctor beforehand to minimize exposure time. You can also use topical anesthetic cream beforehand to numb the area temporarily. The discomfort is usually temporary and resolves quickly — but if the pain persists, let your care team know.

## Q: "What does an IV do?"

**Ages 5–11**
> An IV is a small, thin tube that goes into your vein. It lets the doctor or nurse put medicine right to a spot on your skin — it's like having a little medicine delivery system! It's a quick way for the doctor and nurse to help you feel better, especially if your body needs something extra.

**Ages 12–18**
> An IV (intravenous) line is a thin, flexible tube inserted into a vein. It's used to deliver fluids, medications, and other treatments directly into your bloodstream. The insertion site is usually just above the elbow or in a vein on your hand, and it's secured with tape. IVs are commonly used for monitoring, administering medications, and managing IV fluids.

## Q: "Why do I have to get a blood test?"

**Ages 5–11**
> Getting a blood test is like going on an adventure! It's the hospital doing some really important work to help your body. The blood test is like a special tool that checks how well your heart and lungs are doing, kind of like checking the brakes on a car. It doesn't hurt at all — it just feels like a little poke!

**Ages 12–18**
> Blood tests are a standard diagnostic tool at Children's Hospital. They're used to check your blood chemistry, which can help identify infections (like sepsis), organ damage or inflammation, and other health issues. These can include a complete blood count (CBC), clotting factors, liver function test, and other tests. The results are reviewed by a specialist who decides whether further action is needed.

## Q: "What is Victoria Hospital?"

**Ages 5–11**
> Victoria Hospital is a big, busy place on the second floor of Zone B in London. It's open all day and 24 hours a week, so anyone who needs help can go there. It's like the hospital you know in your home country — it has doctors, nurses, and other helpers who can help with lots of things. If you ever need to go there or have questions about it, your parent can ask the staff!

**Ages 12–18**
> Victoria Hospital is a teaching hospital located at 380 Victoria Road in London. It's part of Western University and offers medical education through its Medical School, the largest in Ontario. The hospital provides a range of medical specialties and emergency services to patients across London. It's a teaching site for medical students and residents — meaning your care is supervised by experienced physicians.

## Q: "Why do I need a needle? I'm scared of needles."

**Ages 5–11**
> It's okay to be scared of needles — it makes sense. The needle is sharp and might feel uncomfortable, but the good news is that most kids here at Children's Hospital are really good at taking care of you. You'll get to see a real nurse who can help with things like drawing blood or using creams on your skin to make it feel less prickly. You might even get a special pair of gloves or slippers that help protect your skin!

**Ages 12–18**
> Fear of needles is a common phobia — it has a lot to do with what needles represent in your brain: sharp, piercing objects that can feel uncomfortable. The fear is also partly about the unknown and unpredictability of what might happen. You have every right to be scared, and it's okay not to go through it without support. If you're anxious about needles specifically, ask your care team if there are any alternative methods or comfort strategies available.

## Q: "When can I go home?"

**Ages 5–11**
> You can go home as soon as your doctor says it's safe. The hospital will tell you when that is — usually around 8 in the morning on a weekday, and it might take some time for your family to get there. Your nurse can help you figure out the best plan, and they'll make sure your family knows when it's okay to leave.

**Ages 12–18**
> You can go home at any time. Your care team will check on you regularly and make sure your safety is being met, but there's no set schedule. If something feels wrong or you're uncomfortable — that's the first thing to say, and your nurse will check on it immediately. If you need a break or want something changed in the unit, let your nurse know right away so they can adjust everything.

## Q: "Why do I have to wear this hospital gown?"

**Ages 5–11**
> Wearing a hospital gown is kind of like wearing your own special pajamas! It's to keep you clean and comfortable while the doctors are taking care of your body. The gown has pockets for things like your armband, slippers or shoes you might wear while in bed. It's also easy for the nurses and doctors at Children's Hospital to see everything going on with you. It might feel a little different, but it doesn't hurt at all!

**Ages 12–18**
> Hospital gowns are designed to protect your body from infection and make you more comfortable while in the hospital. They're usually a full-body garment that covers your clothes, including undergarments and shoes — you'll be wearing slippers or flip-flops. The gown is also a layer of protection against the environment inside and outside your body, which can be sterile. If you have specific concerns about infection prevention or mobility during a procedure, let your nurse know.

## Q: "What do doctors do all day?"

**Ages 5–11**
> Doctors are the experts who help take care of you! They're like superheroes — they make sure your body is working properly and that everything runs smoothly. A doctor might check on how you're doing, help your family figure out what's wrong, and explain everything they do. They might also help your parents decide what's the best plan for you and answer any questions they have. Doctors are always learning so their care keeps getting better every day!

**Ages 12–18**
> Doctors at Children's Hospital work closely with other medical teams to coordinate care. They might be involved in administering tests, ordering imaging studies (like X-rays or CT scans), and reviewing your medical history and test results. They may also participate in consultations with specialists — like paediatricians, surgeons, or psychologists who specialize in children's health. If you have a condition requiring ongoing monitoring, your care team will work across the hospital to ensure you receive consistent quality of care.
